--- layout: page title: Research permalink: /research/ --- Our research is of theoretical and computational nature, and focuses on mesoscale theories of nonequilibrium phenomena in extended systems, with applications to Soft Matter Physics, Materials Science, and Biomechanics. Generically, systems outside of thermodynamic equilibrium involve unstable interfaces and moving topological defects. We develop theories at the mesoscale of the relevant phases, analyze their macroscopic scale asymptotics, and obtain their non equilibrium properties through large scale computation. Our research currently focuses on a self consistent field theory of topological defects in nematic phases in three dimensions, on developing Field Dislocation Mechanics in defected crystalline solids, and on biomechanics of orientationally ordered tissues.

Biomechanics of nematic epithelial tissue

Organogenesis and embryogenesis in living organisms involve large deformations and complex shape changes in tissue that require elaborate mechanical regulation. Recent evidence indicates that the mechanical response of epithelial tissue is strongly anisotropic, with the degree of anisotropy being correlated with the existence of long range orientational order of cytoskeletal organization across the tissue. In order to develop quantitative models of tissue elasticity, theories of ordered, uniaxial, solids are being extended to epithelial tissue, so that order in cytoskeletal organization is
explicitly a dynamical variable that responds to and affects elastic response. With collaborators at the University of Oslo and in Biomedical Engineering, photolithography is being used to pattern living micro tissues with desired shapes, degree of order, and even containing topological defects for analysis. The figure shows experiments involving fibronecting patterning on which cells adhere. Bottom image shows a cell (cyan) migrating in a fibronecting lane (red). Top left shows cells growing on an imprinted substrate; right: phase image of the grown tissue showing individual cell membranes. Theoretical models of tissue development and response, including nematic order are being developed. The image at left shows a model tissue with domains of orientational order (denoted by different colors), including nematic topological defects indicated by arrows and dots. It is possible that orientational order is used to biochemically control the local stiffness or softness of a developing tissue, and hence to facilitate any biologically necessary distortion or shape change. This is a current subject of investigation in fields ranging from embryogenesis to the control of cancer mestastasis. Furthermore, the existence of orientational order naturally brings about the existence of topological defects (disclinations). The biological relevance of disclinations is just beginning to be examined. The figure (left, [T. Saw et al. Nature (2017)]) shows disclinations of positive and negative charge in the cellular arrangement of MDCK tissue. In this particular example, defect core regions are where cell death is triggered, perhaps because of over expression due to localized mechanical stress. The motion of these disclinations can be understood from kinematic laws derived in [CS23] , and the local distortions that accompany tissue stretching [PA25] . The possibility being explored is that mechanical perturbation (say, forces on the boundary of the tissue) can be used to effectively control the motion of defects in the interior, and with them alter the biochemical response or evolution of the tissue. This would effectively allow physical control of biological function.

Spontaneous chiral symmetry breaking in nematics

Chirality (the absence of mirror symmetry) is a common feature of many soft and living systems, and it is a widely used material property in fields such as catalysis and optical sensing. In many systems the molecular units are themselves chiral, a fact that accounts for the appearance of macroscopic handedness. However, the appearance of chirality from centrosymmetric molecular units is a more complex phenomenon as it requires the spontaneous breaking of mirror symmetry, and it is often tied to confining effects. Understanding the mechanisms behind spontaneous chiral symmetry breaking is important in the development and application of related technologies in a number of disciplines. Our focus is on the nematic phase of liquid crystals, systems that can be readily controlled and manipulated experimentally, and are well understood theoretically. Therefore they offer an excellent platform for the quantitative elucidation of spontaneous chiral symmetry breaking.
The ground state of a nematic liquid crystal in a cylindrical geometry, and subject to homeotropic boundary conditions, is being investigated numerically within the tensor order parameter representation [LM25] . A singular potential method is used that allows consideration of defected configurations in three dimensions (containing disclination lines), as well as elastic anisotropy (unequal values of splay, twist, and bend elastic constants). States of broken chiral symmetry are found to be stable for sufficiently small values of the twist elastic constant. For small radii, twisted configurations, which include two disclinations lines that wind around the long axis of the cylinder, are found to minimize the free energy of the nematic. For large radii, non singular (“escaped”) configurations are found to minimize the free energy instead. In this limit, twisted and untwisted configurations are found to be almost degenerate when bend and splay elastic constants are equal. This degeneracy is lifted when bend-splay anisotropy is allowed, with the twisted state becoming of lower free energy.

Self consistent field theory of nematic liquid crystals

The nematic phase of a liquid crystal is an equilibrium phase characterized by long ranged molecular orientational order. Liquid crystal molecules typically have uniaxial symmetry (head-tail symmetry) and therefore allow defects of half integer degree. Shown is a -1/2 disclination in two dimensions (top). The bottom figure shows the probability distribution of orientations on the unit sphere at several points across the disclination region obtained by Self Consistent Field Theory [CS21] . The distribution is uniaxial far from the defect core, becomes biaxial as the core is approached, and becomes again uniaxial right at the core. The degree of order and the structure of the defect core have been typically beyond experimental resolution, and therefore often modeled as effective or point singularities of the (continuous) order parameter field. Recent experiments in lyotropic chromonic liquid crystals have revealed defect core sizes on a scale of a few microns, and have determined their spatial structure in some detail.
Neither of the two current theories in use for the description of nematic phases can address the core structure observed. In one, the Oseen-Frank theory, defects are point singularities of the so called director field, and removed with appropriate cut-off approaches. In the second, the Landau-de Gennes theory, the order parameter at the core is phenomenological, and largely isotropic (in disagreement with experiments). In [CS20a] and [CS20b], we have developed a self consistent field theory of the nematic, which needs to be solved numerically. As in the Landau-de Gennes theory, our approach is based on a tensor order parameter description of the nematic phase. Unlike the latter, we can guarantee that the eigenvalues of the order parameter remain in the physically admissible range throughout the defect, as well as allow different energies for splay and bend distortions while retaining boundedness of the energy. This is also a central feature of the molecular interactions in this lyotropic liquid crystal.
The Self Consistent Field Theory also allows the computation of the equilibrium shapes of "negative tactoids": a domain of the isotropic phase inside a nematic matrix. Shown (left) are experimental (Zhou et al.) images of +1/2 and -1/2 tactoids, and (middle and right) equilibrium shapes given by the theory. Elastic anisotropy is necessary to have domains exhibiting cusps where topology, surface energy, and surface anchoring all balance in precise ways to yield the shapes shown. Biaxiality is present in the interfacial region, and is more pronounced near the cusps where the local anchoring is homeotropic.

Field dislocation mechanics

We are developing a regularized and nanoscale theory of the response of defected solids. It is based on a continuum order parameter description of the material density and distortion that incorporates topological content. Phenomenological equations of motion that respect topological constraints are developed to describe the temporal evolution of an ensemble of interacting defects. We have first shown that our methodology recovers classical results of plasticity theory pertaining to defect motion under stress [AS18] . The figure (left) shows amplitudes and phases of the demodulated order parameter for a dislocation dipole in a hexagonal lattice showing the location and nature of the singularities. Two of three wave amplitudes of the order parameter decay to zero in the defect core region, whereas the phase exhibits a discontinuity along a line joining the cores. These solutions are not imposed as in classical treatments; rather they appear as a direct solution of the nanoscale theory.
The theory has been used to analyze non equilibrium dislocation motion and grain rotation (shown below) [AS18b], showing good agreement with macroscopic theory without requiring any defect core regularization . These results have been further extended to a coarse grained description [MS20], a methodology that alleviates the need to resolve microscopic scales, and allows computations in large systems. We have also clarified shortcomings of previous mesoscale theories of plastic motion which did not properly account for the necessary independence between elastic response, mass transport, and order parameter relaxation [AA20]. This is now a complete model of dislocation evolution as tracked by a phase field.
Finally, nucleation under an imposed stress has also been analyzed [VS20]. The classical incompatibility field is a good indicator of the nucleation event of a topological defect, as well as of the topological content of the emerging defect.
